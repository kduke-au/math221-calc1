The Second Derivative

Read Section 2.5

Introduction

We already have a good understanding of functions with constant slope. These are linear functions, and their graphs are straight lines. If the slope is positive, the function is increasing; if the slope is negative, the function is decreasing.

Over the past few sections, we developed a way to measure the slope of a function when the slope is not constant: the derivative \(f'(x)\) tells us the slope of \(f(x)\) at each point. When the slope of a function is always changing, its graph is no longer a straight line. Instead, the graph bends and curves.

In this section, we will measure how the slope itself is changing. Since \(f'(x)\) is a function, we can take its derivative to find the rate of change of the slope of \(f(x)\).

Definition 1 (The Second Derivative) The second derivative of \(f(x)\), written \(f''(x)\), is the derivative of \(f'(x)\): \[f''(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f'(x+h)-f'(x)}{h}.\] The value \(f''(a)\) measures the instantaneous rate of change of the slope of \(f(x)\) at \(x=a\).

Our goal is to use \(f''(x)\) to understand how the graph of \(f(x)\) is curved, in the same way that we use \(f'(x)\) to understand where \(f(x)\) is increasing or decreasing.

Problem 1 - Motion and Curvature

Sketch a graph for each of the functions described below.

  1. A car is stopped at a stoplight. The light turns green and the car begins to accelerate straight ahead. Let \(A(t)\) be the distance the car travels \(t\) seconds after the light turns green.
  2. A car is headed straight towards the same stoplight on the cross street. The light turns red and the car begins to decelerate. Let \(B(t)\) be the car’s distance from the stoplight \(t\) seconds after the light turns red.
  3. A cyclist is passing through the same intersection as the cars. The cyclist pays no attention to the color of the light and cruises through the intersection with no acceleration or deceleration. Let \(C(t)\) be the distance the cyclist travels \(t\) seconds after the light changes.

Problem 4 - Using the Second Derivative to Assess Estimates

The CFCs in the atmosphere above the US have been reduced from a high of 1915 parts per trillion (ppt) in 2000 to 1640 ppt in 2014. The reduction over this time has been approximately linear. Let \(C(t)\) be the concentration of CFCs in ppt \(t\) years after 2000.

  1. Find \(C(14)\).
  2. Estimate \(C'(14)\).
  3. According to the information given, what is a formula approximating \(C(t)\) on the interval \(0\leq t\leq 14\)?
  4. Use your formula to approximate \(C(20)\).
  5. Suppose that further analysis shows that \(C''(t)<0\) on the interval \(14\leq t\leq 20\). Is your estimate from part d too high or too low? Explain.
