Interpreting Definite Integrals & the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Read Section 5.3

Introduction

So far, we have used Riemann sums to estimate definite integrals, and the area under a graph to compute them exactly when the region has a simple shape. In this section, we answer two questions:

  1. What does a definite integral mean? When the integrand is the rate of change of some quantity, what does the definite integral tell us about that quantity, and in what units?
  2. How can we compute a definite integral exactly when the region under the graph is not a simple shape? Riemann sums only give us estimates, and their limits are hard to calculate directly. Is there a shortcut?

The answer to both questions comes from a connection between definite integrals and derivatives that we have already seen hints of: the signed area under a velocity graph gives the displacement, which is the change in position. This connection is called the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

TipUnits of a Definite Integral (click to expand)

A definite integral is a limit of Riemann sums, which are sums of terms like \(f(t)\,\Delta t\). So its units are \[ (\text{units of } f) \times (\text{units of } t). \] For example, if \(v(t)\) is a velocity in feet per minute and \(t\) is in minutes, then \(\displaystyle\int_a^b v(t)\,dt\) is measured in \(\dfrac{\text{ft}}{\text{min}}\times\text{min} = \text{ft}\).

Checking the units is a quick way to see what a definite integral is measuring before you interpret it.

Discussion Problems

Problem 1 - A Hike

A hiker starts a hike at an initial elevation of 1200 ft. During the first 20 minutes of the hike, their elevation changes at a rate of \(v(t)=18-t\) feet per minute.

  1. True or False: The hiker is always gaining elevation.
  2. Draw a graph of \(v(t)\) on the interval \(0\leq t\leq 20\). Thinking geometrically, use the graph to calculate \(\displaystyle\int_0^{20}v(t)\,dt\). (Hint: Be careful with any part of the region that lies below the \(t\)-axis.)
  3. Let \(h(t)\) be the hiker’s elevation (in feet) \(t\) minutes after the start of the hike.
    1. What is \(h'(t)\)? What is \(h(0)\)?
    2. Use these two facts to guess a formula for \(h(t)\). (Hint: What function has derivative \(18\)? What function has derivative \(t\)? Then, how can you make sure \(h(0)\) is correct?)
    3. Check your guess by taking its derivative and evaluating it at \(t=0\).
  4. Use your formula for \(h(t)\) to calculate the hiker’s change in elevation during the first 20 minutes, \(h(20)-h(0)\). Compare your answer to part (b). Explain why these two values must be the same.
  5. Write a sentence explaining the meaning of the value you found in parts (b) and (d) in the context of the problem.
  6. Determine the maximum elevation the hiker reaches during the first 20 minutes of the hike.
NoteThe Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (click to expand)

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(F'(x)=f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \([a,b]\), then \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a). \]

In words: the definite integral of a rate of change is the total change. If \(F'\) is the rate at which \(F\) changes, then adding up \(F'\) over \([a,b]\) gives how much \(F\) changed between \(a\) and \(b\).

In Problem 1: the rate of change of the hiker’s elevation is \(h'(t)=v(t)\), so \[ \int_0^{20} v(t)\,dt = h(20)-h(0). \] The left side is the signed area you found geometrically in part (b), and the right side is the change in elevation you found with your formula in part (d).

Why this is useful: to compute \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) exactly, we don’t need to find any areas or limits of Riemann sums. We only need a function \(F\) whose derivative is \(f\). A function like this is called an antiderivative of \(f\).

Problem 3 - What Does It Mean?

Write a sentence explaining each of the following equations:

  1. \(\displaystyle\int_0^6 a(t)\,dt=50\), where \(a(t)\) is acceleration in m/s\(^2\) and \(t\) is time in seconds.
  2. \(\displaystyle\int_0^5 s(x)\,dx = 38\), where \(s(x)\) is the rate of change of the salinity (salt concentration) in gm/liter per cm of sea water \(x\) cm below the surface of the water.
  3. \(\displaystyle p(4)+\int_4^9 p'(t)\,dt = 30\), where \(p'(t)\) is the rate of change of a company’s stock price (in $ per share per day) \(t\) days after January 1, 2018.
  4. \(\displaystyle\frac{1}{9-4}\int_4^9 p'(t)\,dt=-0.5\), where \(p'(t)\) is the rate of change of a company’s stock price (in $ per share per day) \(t\) days after January 1, 2018.
TipA Strategy for Interpreting Definite Integrals (click to expand)
  1. Identify the rate. What quantity is the integrand the rate of change of? What are its units?
  2. Identify the interval. What do the limits of integration represent (times, depths, distances, …)?
  3. Find the units of the integral: (units of the integrand) \(\times\) (units of the variable).
  4. Write the sentence. “Between [start] and [end], [the quantity] changed by [value] [units].” Make sure to say whether it increased or decreased.

Note: Be careful to distinguish between the amount of a quantity and the change in that quantity. By the Fundamental Theorem, a definite integral of a rate gives a change. To find an amount, you also need to know a starting value, like in part (c).
