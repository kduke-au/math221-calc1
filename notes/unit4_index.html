Applications of the Derivative

Unit 4 Overview

Introduction

In Unit 2, we developed the concept of the derivative as an instantaneous rate of change. In Unit 3, we developed rules that let us compute derivatives quickly. In this unit, we combine these two to see how we can use derivatives to try and understand several different types of applied problems.

Many questions about a changing quantity can be answered using derivatives. In this unit, we focus on the following questions:

  1. How fast? If two quantities are related and one of them is changing, how fast is the other one changing?
  2. About how much? If we know the value of a function and its rate of change at one input, can we estimate its value at nearby inputs?
  3. What shape? What do the first and second derivatives tell us about where the graph of a function rises, falls, and bends? Or, if we know what shape we want a function to have, can we select the right parameters to match those criteria?
  4. What’s best? Where does a function reach its largest or smallest value?

The main challenge in this unit is usually not computing a derivative. Instead, it is modeling: translating a situation into functions and equations, deciding which application of derivatives answers the question being asked, and interpreting the result in the proper context.

For example, consider the following problem:

Problem 1 - Building a Box

A box with an open top is made from a 12 in \(\times\) 12 in square sheet of cardboard by cutting a square with side length \(x\) inches out of each corner and folding up the sides.

  1. Sketch the sheet with the corners removed and label the dimensions of the finished box in terms of \(x\). Write a formula for the volume \(V(x)\) of the box. What values of \(x\) make sense in this context?

  2. How fast? Imagine an animation in which the cut size \(x\) grows steadily at a rate of 0.5 inches per second. How fast is the volume of the box changing at the moment when \(x=1\)? (Hint: What does \(V'(1)\) measure, and what are its units?)

  3. About how much? Find \(V(1)\) and \(V'(1)\). Use these two values, without plugging \(1.1\) into your formula for \(V\), to estimate \(V(1.1)\). Then compute \(V(1.1)\) directly. How good was your estimate? Why do you think it worked as well as it did?

  4. What shape? For which values of \(x\) is \(V\) increasing? Decreasing? Explain, in terms of the physical box, why the volume can’t keep increasing forever as \(x\) grows.

  5. What’s best? What cut size gives the box with the largest possible volume? How do you know your answer is the largest, and not just larger than the values you happened to try?
